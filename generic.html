About Me :)

I grew up in California, surrounded by mountains, coastlines, and constant reminders of how alive and changeable the world is. The landscape was always in motion — hills turning green with winter rain and fading to gold by summer, rivers shrinking, and fires reshaping entire ecosystems overnight. Living in a place that changes so visibly made me pay attention. It taught me that the environment isn’t static; it’s always responding, adapting, and recovering. That awareness slowly turned into curiosity about how living things, especially insects, adjust to those same changes.

As a kid, I was fascinated by the small details of nature — the patterns of ants on sidewalks, the colors of tide pools, and how each creature seemed to have a rhythm that made sense in its own world. I didn’t know it then, but those small observations were my first lessons in ecology and behavior. They made me want to understand how movement, both individual and collective, connects to survival.

Now I’m a PhD student in Organismic and Evolutionary Biology at Harvard University, where I study insect locomotion and collective behavior. My research combines computational tools and remote sensing to understand how movement reflects the environments animals live in — how behavior, ecology, and evolution are linked across scales. I’m especially interested in how disturbances like wildfire influence the ways insects move and interact. What I love most about this work is that it lets me connect detailed, ground-level behavior with broad ecological patterns — studying life both up close and from above.

Before graduate school, I worked on projects that deepened my interest in how environmental change shapes behavior — from field studies to computational analyses. Those experiences reinforced for me that science isn’t just about collecting data; it’s about learning to look closely, to be creative, and to care about the systems we’re part of.

Outside of research, I like to spend my time cooking, hiking, and working on creative projects. I love sharing meals with friends, exploring trails around New England, and making things just for the joy of it — whether that’s baking something new, learning a skill, or fixing something around the house. I’ve always needed balance — space for focus and curiosity in my research, and time for rest and play in everything else. Being creative in small, everyday ways helps me recharge and stay connected to the people and places around me. It’s also a reminder that curiosity doesn’t just belong in science; it can shape how we live, cook, build, and notice the world, too.
